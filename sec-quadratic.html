    Section 3.2 Quadratic Functions

    A quadratic function has the standard form \(ax^2+bx+c\text{.}\) We will focus on expanding and factoring quadratics.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2.1 Expanding Quadratics

    If we have a quadratic in a factored form, it will have the form \((ax+b)(cx+d)\text{.}\) When expanding, we wish to write it in the standard form. When each set of brackets has two terms, we must multiply the first (F) terms in each bracket, the outer (O) terms, the inner (I) terms, and the last (L) terms. This is often remembered using the acronym FOIL. This gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (ax)(cx)+(ax)(d)+(b)(cx)+(b)(d)=acx^2+(ad+bc)x+bd. \end{equation*}

    Example 3.2.1. Expanding Quadratics.

    Expand \((3x-5)(2x+7)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We follow the FOIL acronym, giving
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (3x-5)(2x+7)=(3x)(2x)+(3x)(7)+(-5)(2x)+(-5)(7). \end{equation*}
    Note that here the term \(3x-5\) was treated as \(3x+(-5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We can then simplify further to give
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 6x^2+21x-10x-35=6x^2+11x-35. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2.2 Factoring Quadratics

    We now wish to go the other way. Given the standard form \(ax^2+bx+c\text{,}\) we will factor the quadratic to the form \((mx+n)(px+q)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note that we know how to expand \((mx+n)(px+q)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (mx+n)(px+q)=mpx^2+(mq+np)x+np. \end{equation*}
    If this is the same as \(ax^2+bx+c\text{,}\) then we must have \(mp=a\text{,}\) \(mq+np=b\text{,}\) and \(nq=c\text{.}\) With a bit of trial and error, we can determine the values of m, n, p, and q.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.3. Factoring Quadratics.

    Factor \(15x^2-5x-10\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The factored form will be \((mx+n)(px+q)\) where
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} mp=15, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} mq+np=-5, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} nq=-10. \end{equation*}
    If m and p are integers (whole numbers), then they are divisors of 15. We could have 1 and 15 or 3 and 5. Similarly, if n and q are integers, then they are divisors of -10. We could have -1 and 10, 1 and -10, -2 and 5, or 2 and -5.
    🔗
    We now consider what will result in \(mq+np=-5\text{.}\) If we have 15 multiplied by 2, -2, 5, -5, 10, or -10, this will be too large. We can test the possibilities where 15 is multiplied by 1 or -1:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (15x+10)(x-1)=15x^2-5x-10, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} (15x-10)(x+1)=15x^2+5x-10. \end{equation*}
    Neither of these is correct, so m and p must be 3 and 5 in some order. The order does not matter, so we will assume that \(m=3\) and \(p=5\text{.}\) Now we require
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3q+5n=-5. \end{equation*}
    Only one of our options for n and q works, \(q=-5\) and \(n=2\text{.}\) This tells us that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 15x^2-5x-10=(3x+2)(5x-5). \end{equation*}
    Note that you can always check your final answer by expanding!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
